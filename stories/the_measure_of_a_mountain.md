# The Measure of a Mountain

> **Osvald & Partitio** · the Wildlands · ~2,444 words  
> Opening: *These two travelers decided to go on a trip together.*  
> Written by Claude Opus 4.6, checked against the knowledge base by the validator.

The golt cheese had gone rancid three days into the prospecting trip, and Osvald counted this as evidence that optimism was a poor substitute for planning.

He stood at the edge of a nameless canyon in the Western Gravell Wilds, squinting through the sulfurous haze at the rock face opposite. The volcanic stone here was darker than the red deposits near Crackridge, shot through with veins of something that glittered faintly in the harsh midday sun. Partitio had insisted it was silver. Osvald had his doubts.

"Now that's what I call a view!" Partitio appeared at his elbow, thumbs hooked in his vest pockets, fedora pushed back on his head. The merchant was grinning despite the heat, the dust, and the fact that their waterskins were half-empty. "Look at all that potential just waiting to be tapped."

Osvald adjusted his spectacles and said nothing. The canyon was approximately forty meters across at this point, the depth difficult to estimate without proper instruments. The air rising from below carried the acrid tang of sulfur dioxide, suggesting active volcanic vents. Potential, perhaps. Also danger.

"You see those veins?" Partitio gestured broadly at the far wall. "Silver, or I'm a dancing bear. High-grade, too, judging by the color. We could stake a claim right here, partner. Think what that'd do for Gravell—folks up there could use the work."

"Hmm." Osvald stroked his beard. The village of Gravell had indeed fallen on hard times, a pattern of calamity and decline that predated even the Moonshade Order's machinations. But Partitio's enthusiasm had a way of glossing over practical obstacles. "And how do you propose we cross?"

"Well now, that's the question, isn't it?" Partitio removed his hat and wiped his brow with his sleeve, replacing the hat at a jaunty angle. "I was thinking you might have some scholarly insight on that front."

Of course he was. Osvald turned from the canyon edge and walked back to where they had dropped their packs against a heat-cracked boulder. He rummaged in his own pack—organized, each item in its designated place—and withdrew a length of rope, a metal spike, and a small hammer.

"The width is manageable," he said. "Forty meters, perhaps forty-two. The wind shear here is inconsistent." He glanced up at the sky, calculating. "Midafternoon thermals rising from the canyon floor. We would need to account for lateral drift."

"So we can do it?" Partitio had that light in his eyes, the one that appeared whenever he smelled opportunity.

"I did not say that."

Osvald crouched and began working the spike into a crack in the stone, testing the rock's integrity. Volcanic basalt, cooled quickly, which meant brittleness in the upper layers. The hammer rang against the spike in steady, measured strikes. Behind him, Partitio paced, boots scuffing the hardpan.

"You know," the merchant said, "Papp always told me a man's reach should exceed his grasp. That's how progress happens, y'see? Someone's gotta be the first to try."

"And someone has to calculate whether that first attempt will end in success or a forty-meter fall." The spike seated with a final blow. Osvald tugged it, testing. It held, but he did not trust it. "The problem is not the crossing. The problem is whether there is sufficient silver in that seam to justify the risk."

Partitio laughed, a warm sound that seemed incongruous in this blasted landscape. "Mister Osvald, that's the difference between a scholar and a merchant. You want certainty before you act. Me, I'm comfortable with a calculated gamble."

"Calculated." Osvald stood and fixed the younger man with a flat look. "You have calculated, then, the tensile strength of this rope relative to the combined weight of two men and their equipment? The likelihood of finding suitable anchor points on both sides? The market price of silver relative to the cost of extraction in an unmapped deposit?"

"Well, no, but—"

"Then your gamble is uncalculated. Which makes it foolish." He began coiling the rope again. "We turn back."

For a moment Partitio's perpetual good humor flickered. His jaw set in a way Osvald recognized—the merchant preparing to dig in his heels. They had done this dance before, in New Delsta and Montwise, though usually with Osvald as the intransigent one. He waited.

"Now hold on," Partitio said slowly. "We came out here together, didn't we? You agreed to this trip."

"I agreed to travel with you through the Wildlands. Not to hurl myself into a canyon on the strength of your optimism."

"Is that what you think this is?" A faint edge had entered Partitio's drawl. "Me being reckless?"

Osvald considered the question. No, that was not quite accurate. Partitio was many things—impulsive, generous to a fault, incurably hopeful—but he was not reckless. The man had restored Oresrush from destitution, purchased steam engine rights for eighty billion leaves, and turned a betrayer into a business partner. Those were not the acts of a fool.

"No," he admitted.

"Then what's really stopping you?"

The wind gusted across the canyon, carrying a fresh wave of sulfur. Osvald's throat tightened with the smell, or perhaps with something else. What was stopping him? The answer formed with the precision of a mathematical proof, each premise building on the last.

This was Gravell. A handful of kilometers north, the Duskruin Shrine sat beneath the earth like a wound that would not heal. He had walked those passages, fought Harvey in the dark, torn his daughter free from shadow. He had achieved the One True Magic there—love made manifest—and nearly lost everything in the same moment. The Wildlands themselves smelled of that night: sulfur and stone and desperation.

He had not wanted to return. But Partitio had asked, and Osvald had found himself unable to refuse. Some debt of companionship, perhaps. Or maybe just the recognition that a friend's enthusiasm could be a kind of anchor, pulling him forward when grief would otherwise have rooted him in place.

"I do not like this place," Osvald said quietly.

Partitio's expression shifted, the salesman's brightness giving way to something gentler. "Yeah," he said. "I figured."

They stood in silence. A dust devil spun across the canyon floor far below, red stone and ash churning in a tight spiral before dissipating against the far wall. Osvald watched it with a scholar's detachment, cataloging the vectors and velocities, anything to avoid examining the real equation.

"You know," Partitio said eventually, "I don't actually care about the silver."

Osvald glanced at him sharply.

"Oh, I thought I did when we set out." Partitio grinned, a little rueful. "Had this whole vision—new prosperity for Gravell, second boom, Yellowil Trading Company logo on every bag of ore. But standing here now, I'm thinking that was just... an excuse."

"An excuse for what?"

"To spend some time with a friend." Partitio resettled his hat. "You've been holed up in Conning Creek for months, Mister Osvald. Ever since we put Vide down and you brought Elena home. Figured you could use a change of scenery."

Something uncomfortable lodged in Osvald's chest. He was unaccustomed to being the object of concern. "I have been occupied with Elena's recovery. And my research."

"Sure, sure. Important work, no doubt about it." Partitio's tone was mild. "But even a scholar's gotta come up for air sometimes, y'see? Can't spend every hour locked in a study."

"I am not locked anywhere. I am a free man." The words came out more sharply than Osvald intended. The shackle around his neck—still there, after all this time—seemed to tighten.

Partitio held up his hands, placating. "Didn't mean it like that, partner. Just... seemed like you were carrying something heavy. Thought maybe the road would help lighten the load."

Osvald turned away, ostensibly to examine the canyon again. In truth, he needed a moment to master the emotion rising in his throat. Partitio saw too much. It was one of the man's talents, that ability to read people and their needs, to know when to push and when to simply stand beside them.

"Elena remembers everything now," Osvald said. The words came haltingly, drawn up from some depth he preferred not to examine. "Her mother. The fire. Harvey's manipulations. She is recovering, but the process is..." He adjusted his spectacles. "There are no equations for grief. I cannot calculate the correct words to say to her. I cannot prove that I will not fail her again."

"You didn't fail her the first time," Partitio said firmly. "Harvey did that. Not you."

"I was imprisoned. Unable to protect her."

"Because you were framed for a murder you didn't commit." Partitio stepped closer, his shadow falling across the stone between them. "Mister Osvald, you tore through a frozen wasteland, escaped a prison fortress, hunted down a madman, and brought your little girl home alive. That's not failure by any measure I know."

"She was already harmed when I found her."

"And you saved her before worse could happen." Partitio's voice was gentle but unyielding. "You can't undo what Harvey did. Nobody can. But you're there for her now, and that's what matters. That's what she'll remember when all this is further behind her."

Osvald wanted to argue, to enumerate all the ways he had been insufficient, but the merchant's logic was as sound as any proof. Elena was safe. She was healing. She knew him as her father again and asked him to teach her magic, as she once had when she was small. These were facts. The rest was merely his own inability to forgive himself.

"Hmm," he said.

Partitio grinned. "I'll take that as agreement."

Despite himself, Osvald felt the corner of his mouth twitch. "You are insufferable."

"Been told that before." Partitio clapped him on the shoulder, a gesture Osvald tolerated from very few people. "So what do you say? We done gambling on silver, or do you want to prove me right and find a way across?"

Osvald looked at the canyon again. The veins of ore glittered in the afternoon light, a promise of wealth or merely a trick of mica. Without proper analysis, there was no way to be certain. And yet Partitio stood here, thirty kilometers from anywhere, waiting on his answer with that unshakeable good humor.

The man was not actually asking about the silver, Osvald realized. He was asking whether Osvald was ready to move forward. Whether the Wildlands could be just another landscape instead of a monument to his worst moments.

"The angle is wrong," Osvald said slowly. "We would need to move west, find where the canyon narrows. There is a geological principle—erosion patterns suggest the canyon walls converge approximately one kilometer in that direction."

Partitio's grin widened. "Now that's the spirit! I knew you'd have a plan."

"I have a hypothesis. There is a difference."

"We'll test it then. That's what you scholarly types do, right? Test and measure and prove?" Partitio was already hefting his pack. "Come on, partner. Daylight's burning and we've got ground to cover."

Osvald shouldered his own pack, settling the familiar weight across his shoulders. The rope, the hammer, the spike—tools for measuring and building and making safe passage across empty space. He had learned on Frigit Isle how to make use of what little was at hand. He had learned in Gravell that love could be a weapon against darkness.

Perhaps now he could learn that a canyon was only a canyon.

They walked west along the rim, Partitio whistling some jaunty tune that probably had words about prosperity and friendship. Osvald ignored it, focused instead on the rock formations, calculating the rate of erosion, estimating where the canyon would pinch. The numbers were steady. Reliable. They did not ask questions he could not answer.

"You know," Partitio said after a while, "when we get back, you should bring Elena out to Oresrush. Little one ought to see where her old man's been traveling. Plus Papp makes a mean goulash—probably not as good as your Rita's, but it'd be a sight better than what they serve in Conning Creek."

Osvald's step faltered. The easy invocation of Rita's name, the casual assumption that Elena would want to travel, that Osvald would want to introduce her to his companions—it was too much and not enough all at once.

But Partitio was not pressing. He simply walked, hands in his pockets, confident that the invitation stood whether Osvald answered now or not.

"Perhaps," Osvald said finally. "She has been asking about the other travelers."

"There you go! We'll make it a regular gathering. Get Castti to cook something that won't poison us, convince Agnea to teach the kid some dance steps. It'll be good for everyone."

The canyon curved, and Osvald could see the far wall beginning to angle inward. His hypothesis was proving correct. The gap here was thirty meters at most, perhaps less. And there—yes, a natural stone bridge, half-collapsed but navigable with care.

"There," he said, pointing.

Partitio shaded his eyes and let out a low whistle. "Well I'll be. Mister Osvald, you just saved us a heap of trouble."

They picked their way down the slope toward the bridge. The stone was weathered but solid, basalt cooled in some ancient eruption and scoured by centuries of wind. Osvald tested each step before committing his weight, and Partitio followed without complaint.

Halfway across, Osvald paused. The canyon yawned beneath them, deep enough that the bottom was lost in shadow. But the walls here were close, and the bridge was sound. He could see the path forward.

"Thank you," he said, not looking back.

"For what?" Partitio's voice held genuine confusion.

"For the change of scenery."

Behind him, Partitio laughed. "Anytime, partner. Anytime."

They reached the far side as the sun began its descent toward the western plateaus, painting the volcanic stone in shades of amber and rust. The veins Partitio had spotted from across the canyon proved to be mostly quartz, threaded with only modest traces of silver. Enough to note the location, perhaps, but not enough to justify a mining operation.

"Well," Partitio said, crouching to examine the rock, "can't win 'em all."

"You are not disappointed."

"Nah." The merchant stood and dusted off his hands. "Got what I came for."

Osvald did not ask him to elaborate. Some things resisted definition, and he had learned—was still learning—that this was acceptable. They had crossed a canyon together. They would walk back to Gravell, and then to Oresrush, and perhaps someday Osvald would bring Elena to see this harsh, beautiful place and tell her that her father had friends here.

The equation balanced. For now, that was enough.
