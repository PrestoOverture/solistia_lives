# Red Dust Fever

> **Castti & Partitio** · Crackridge · ~2,269 words  
> Opening: *A sickness is spreading in town; Castti has come to treat it, and Partitio is here on business.*  
> Written by Claude Opus 4.6, checked against the knowledge base by the validator.

The wind came first, like it always did in Crackridge—sudden and sharp, whipping grit against Castti's cheek as she knelt beside the miner's cot. She'd learned to blink through it in the three days since she'd arrived. The red dust got everywhere: under fingernails, in the seams of her apron, coating the inside of her nose until each breath tasted of iron and stone.

"How long has he been feverish?" she asked, pressing two fingers to the man's wrist. His pulse ran too fast, and his skin had taken on a grayish cast beneath the tan.

The miner's wife twisted her hands in her apron. "Four days now. Started with the cough, then the fever came on. I gave him the tea like you said, but—"

"You did well." Castti kept her voice calm even as she noted the shallow rasp of the man's breathing. "Keep him cool. I'll prepare a stronger decoction."

Outside the modest house, she paused to count. Seventeen cases now, all quarry workers or their families. The pattern was clear enough: it started in the lungs, a dry cough that turned wet and rattling, then fever, then a dangerous weakness that left grown men unable to stand. Two had died before she'd arrived. She'd managed to stabilize the rest, but new cases appeared each day.

The inn stood at the center of town, its impeccable reputation intact despite the crisis. Castti had claimed a corner of the common room as her staging area, and the staff had obliged without complaint, clearing tables and boiling water at all hours. Her satchel rested on a bench, bottles and pouches arranged in careful rows.

She was grinding coriander root when boots scuffed behind her.

"Well now, this is a sight I wasn't expectin'."

Castti turned, mortar in hand, and felt her mouth curve into a smile. Partitio Yellowil stood in the doorway, his yellow coat vivid against the drab stone, his white fedora dusty from the road. He looked exactly as he had when they'd parted ways in Gravell two months prior: optimistic, energetic, and slightly sunburned.

"Partitio. What brings you to Crackridge?"

"Business!" He crossed the room in three long strides, grinning. "Came to talk red stone with the quarry foremen. This stuff's worth its weight in silver once it's ground fine, and I've got a notion about standardizin' the pigment for painters across Solistia. Create a reliable supply, see, and—" He stopped, taking in the medicinal supplies spread across the table, the smell of herbs steeping in the corner. "What's all this?"

"An outbreak. Respiratory, probably from the quarry dust." She returned to grinding, the rhythmic scrape a comfort. "Seventeen cases so far."

His expression shifted, the easy cheer draining away. "Anyone—?"

"Two deaths before I arrived. No more since." She kept her voice matter-of-fact. No use dwelling on the ones she couldn't save. "The patients are stable, but I haven't identified the root cause. Until I do, I can only treat the symptoms."

Partitio pulled out a chair and sat, elbows on his knees. "What do you need?"

She glanced up. "Need?"

"Supplies, help, another pair of hands—whatever'll get these folks back on their feet." He met her eyes, and there was no hesitation in his face, just that same earnest certainty she'd seen when he'd handed silver coins to strangers and promised them better days. "I'm here. Put me to work."

Something warm settled in her chest. She'd worked alone after Healeaks, after losing Malaya and the others. Having someone offer help so freely, no questions asked, felt like stepping into sunlight after a long winter.

"The patients need fluids," she said. "Clean water, broth if they can take it. And someone should check on the families, make sure they're eating."

"Consider it done." He stood, tipping his hat. "You just focus on the curin'. I'll handle the rest."

He was as good as his word. By evening, Castti found him at the bedside of a young quarry worker, coaxing the man to sip from a cup of broth. Partitio kept up a steady stream of conversation—stories about Oresrush, about his father Papp's railway plans, about a gramophone he'd helped bring to market—and the patient actually smiled, weak though it was.

"You're good at this," Castti said when Partitio stepped outside.

"Aw, I'm just talkin'." He rubbed the back of his neck. "Figured if they're gonna be stuck in bed, might as well give 'em somethin' to think about besides feelin' poorly."

"That's more valuable than you know." She fell into step beside him as they walked toward the next house. The sun was setting, painting the red cliffs in shades of crimson and gold. "Keeping their spirits up helps them fight the illness."

"Then I'm happy to jabber all day long." He paused at a fork in the dusty street. "Say, you eaten anything since breakfast?"

She tried to remember. Had she eaten breakfast? "I'll have something later."

"That's what you said at lunch." His drawl sharpened with exasperation. "C'mon now, you can't take care of folks if you're runnin' on empty. Even a steam engine needs fuel."

"I'm fine—"

"Really now?" She'd used that tone on him once before, back when he'd insisted he was fine after three days of nonstop negotiations. His grin flickered. "All right, all right. But I'm bringin' you somethin' from the inn, and you're gonna eat it while you work. Deal?"

She relented. "Deal."

True to form, he returned with bread, cheese, and a bowl of stew that was better than it had any right to be. She ate while reviewing her notes, and Partitio sprawled in the chair across from her, working his way through a similar meal.

"So," he said, breaking off a piece of bread, "what's got you stumped?"

"The illness presents like pneumoconiosis—lung disease from inhaling stone dust. That's common in miners. But this is spreading too fast, and the treatment isn't working as well as it should." She tapped her pencil against her journal. "There's something else at play. Something I'm missing."

Partitio chewed thoughtfully. "How long do these folks usually work in the quarries before they get sick?"

"Years, typically. Why?"

"Well, three of the fellas I talked to today were new hires. Started less than two months back." He pulled a silver coin from his pocket and rolled it across his knuckles, a thinking habit she'd noticed before. "If it was just the dust, wouldn't the old-timers be worse off?"

Castti straightened. "You're right. The veteran miners should be the most affected, but they're not." She flipped back through her notes, checking the cases. Partitio was right—more than half the patients had been hired in the last three months. "They're working a new section of the quarry. They must have broken into a seam with different minerals."

"Could it be poison?"

"Not quite. More likely a mineral that becomes toxic when ground into dust." Her mind raced through possibilities. Silica, arsenic compounds, certain clays. "I need to see the quarry."

"Then let's go." He stood, settling his hat.

"Now? It's nearly dark."

"You got a better time? Sooner we figure this out, sooner we can stop it." He offered his hand to pull her up. "Besides, I've got a lantern."

The quarry foreman was less enthusiastic about their nighttime visit, but Partitio's charm and a few well-placed silver coins convinced him to unlock the gates. The quarry itself was a gash in the cliff face, terraced levels cut deep into the red stone. Lantern light flickered across pickaxes and hand carts abandoned for the night.

"New seam's down on the third level," the foreman said, leading them along a narrow path. "Richest red we've struck in years. Stone practically glows when you grind it."

Castti's unease grew with each step down. The air felt wrong here, heavy and close despite the wind. When they reached the third terrace, she knelt and examined the exposed rock face. The stone was darker than the surrounding layers, shot through with green-black veins.

"This isn't just red stone," she said. "There's cinnabar mixed in. Mercury ore."

Partitio crouched beside her. "That bad?"

"Very. When they cut and grind it, mercury dust gets into the air. Into their lungs." She stood, brushing off her hands. "That's why the treatment wasn't working. I've been treating pneumoconiosis, but this is mercury poisoning."

The foreman paled. "We'll have to shut down the whole section. Lose weeks of work—"

"You'll lose more than that if your workers die." Castti's voice hardened. "This seam needs to be sealed until proper ventilation can be installed. And every worker who's been in contact with it needs to be examined."

"Now hold on—"

"She's right." Partitio's easygoing manner had vanished. "You're sittin' on a fortune here, friend, but what good's wealth if you're buryin' your workers to get it? Close the seam, install some airshafts, wet down the dust before cuttin'. Do it right, and you'll still make your profit. Do it wrong, and word'll get around that Crackridge stone costs lives. Then who'll buy?"

The foreman worked his jaw. "Fine. But you're tellin' the owners."

"Happy to." Partitio's grin returned. "In fact, I'll even help y'all source the equipment. Know a fellow in Oresrush who makes excellent ventilation systems for mines. Give you a fair price, too."

Despite herself, Castti smiled. Trust Partitio to turn a crisis into a business opportunity.

Back at the inn, she prepared new treatments—sodium thiosulfate to bind the mercury, plus supportive care for the lungs. Partitio made the rounds with her, explaining to each family what had happened and what came next. He had a gift for it, she realized. He could deliver hard news without crushing hope, could talk about illness and recovery in the same breath and make people believe the recovery was coming.

It was past midnight when they finally returned to the inn. Most of the lamps had been extinguished, and the common room stood empty except for the two of them. Castti sank into a chair, exhaustion washing over her in waves.

"Long day," Partitio said, sliding into the seat across from her.

"Long three days." She managed a tired smile. "Thank you for your help. I couldn't have identified the source without you."

"Aw, you'd have figured it out." He waved a hand. "I just asked a couple questions."

"You asked the right questions. And you knew how to talk to the foreman." She met his eyes. "You've done this before. Convinced someone to do the right thing even when it cost them money."

His expression turned serious. "Back in Oresrush, when things got bad, folks started takin' shortcuts. Dangerous shortcuts. I learned then that you gotta show people there's another way, a better way, or they'll just keep doin' what they've always done." He fiddled with his hat. "Sometimes the right thing and the profitable thing are the same. You just gotta help folks see it."

Castti thought of Trousseau, who'd stopped seeing the value in saving lives. Who'd looked at suffering and called death a mercy. Partitio was his opposite in every way—someone who looked at suffering and saw problems to solve, people to help, a future worth building.

"You're going to change Solistia," she said quietly.

He laughed, but it was gentle. "That's a tall order."

"You're already doing it. One town, one deal, one person at a time." She pulled a small vial from her satchel and set it on the table between them. "Skybalm. For the road. In case you need it."

He picked up the vial, turning it in the lamplight. "You sure? This stuff's valuable."

"So's a friend who drops everything to help sick strangers." She stood, shouldering her satchel. "Get some rest, Partitio. Tomorrow we check on the patients and make sure the quarry stays closed."

"Yes ma'am." He tipped his hat, grinning. "Though I gotta say, I like workin' with you. You've got a good head for problems."

"So do you." She paused at the base of the stairs. "When this is over, if you're ever near Healeaks, stop by. I'm rebuilding Eir's Apothecaries there. We could use someone with your skills—someone who knows how to talk to people, how to organize relief efforts."

His eyes widened. "You're offerin' me a job?"

"I'm offering a partnership. When disaster strikes, you'll know how to mobilize resources and I'll know how to treat the wounded. Together we could save a lot of lives."

He was quiet for a moment, and she could see him turning it over in his mind, weighing possibilities. Then he smiled, warm as summer sun. "I'd be honored. Truly. Let me finish this railway business with my pa, and then I'll come find you. We'll work out the details."

"I'll hold you to that."

As she climbed the stairs to her room, Castti felt something she hadn't felt since before Healeaks: the sense that she wasn't alone in this work. Malaya and the others were gone, but new allies were emerging. Good people, like Partitio, who saw the same suffering she did and refused to look away.

Tomorrow there would be more patients to treat, more families to reassure, more work to do. But tonight, for the first time in days, she let herself believe that the outbreak would end, that the miners would recover, that Crackridge would learn from this and do better.

The wind rattled her window, carrying red dust and the faint smell of stone. Castti set her satchel by the bed, checked her supplies one last time, and finally let herself rest.
