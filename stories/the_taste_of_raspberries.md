# The Taste of Raspberries

> **Agnea & Throné** · Cropdale · ~2,412 words  
> Opening: *Agnea has brought Throné to a festival here.*  
> Written by Claude Opus 4.6, checked against the knowledge base by the validator.

The festival lanterns swayed in the evening breeze, casting warm light across the wooden stage where Agnea had once danced in her mother's dress. Throné stood at the edge of the Festival Grounds, back to a oak tree, watching villagers mill between food stalls. The scent of baking pies drifted through the air—peach, mostly, but also raspberry.

Agnea had insisted she come. "You've got to see Cropdale during festival season," she'd said in New Delsta, eyes bright. "It's not the Grand Gala, but it's home, and I want you to meet everyone." Throné had meant to refuse. Crowds, noise, strangers wanting conversation—all things she preferred to avoid. But Agnea's smile had been so hopeful, and after everything they'd been through together in the darkness beneath the world, it had seemed wrong to say no.

Now she was here, and Agnea had vanished into the throng of well-wishers twenty minutes ago. Throné scanned the crowd with professional efficiency: exits clear, no suspicious movement, nobody watching her. Good. She shifted her weight, felt the reassuring absence where her collar used to rest. Free. The word still felt strange.

"Throné!" Agnea stumbled through the crowd, nearly tripping over her own feet before catching herself on a stall post. She carried two wooden cups, raspberry juice sloshing dangerously close to the rims. "Sorry, sorry! Mrs. Keeland wouldn't stop talking about her prize-winning pie, and then Papa wanted to show me the new bolts of fabric he'd ordered, and—oh!" She thrust one cup forward. "I got you this."

Throné accepted the cup, studying the dark red liquid. "Raspberry?"

"You like them, don't you?" Agnea beamed. "I remembered you saying so once. We've got the best raspberry orchards in the Leaflands. Maybe all of Solistia."

The juice was cold, tart-sweet, and Throné found herself drinking more than she'd intended. "It's good."

"Right?" Agnea sipped her own, leaving a faint red stain on her upper lip. "I used to pick them with Pala when we were little. Mama would make raspberry tarts, and Papa would pretend to scold us for eating half the harvest." Her smile softened. "Those were good days."

A child's laughter rang out nearby. Throné watched a young girl chase her brother between the stalls, their mother calling after them with mock sternness. The Festival Grounds felt nothing like the cramped, tense spaces of New Delsta. Here, people smiled without calculation. They gathered to celebrate fruit and song, not because they feared what would happen if they didn't.

"You're thinking something," Agnea said quietly. She'd learned to read Throné's silences during their journey. "What is it?"

"Just that this place is very different from where I grew up." Throné drained her cup. "Nobody here looks like they're checking exits or counting valuables."

"Well, we do have that one woman who curses people from the bridge," Agnea offered, then laughed at her own joke. "But mostly, yeah. Cropdale's gentle. I didn't really understand that until I left and saw other places." She glanced at Throné, her expression turning more thoughtful. "Do you think you'll find it? That place you talked about once. The one that's not on any map."

Throné had mentioned it exactly once, on a cold night in the Wildlands when the eight of them had sat around a fire. Agnea had better memory than people gave her credit for. "Eventually. When I'm ready."

"I hope it's as peaceful as this." Agnea gestured at the grounds with her cup. "Maybe even a little boring. You deserve boring, Throné."

Something in Throné's chest tightened. Boring. When had anyone wished that for her before? Not excitement, not glory, not even safety exactly, but simple, unremarkable peace. "Thank you."

Agnea smiled, then suddenly straightened. "Oh! The baking contest is about to start. Come on, you have to see this—people get really serious about their pies."

"I'll watch from here."

"Throne." Agnea fixed her with a look that was surprisingly firm for someone who tripped over her own feet. "You came all the way to Cropdale. You can come twenty more steps to the contest tent."

"I don't do well in crowds."

"I know." Agnea's voice gentled. "But you're not in New Delsta anymore. Nobody here knows you, and nobody's watching you. Except me, and I'm just trying to show you something nice." She held out her hand. "Please?"

Throné looked at the offered hand, then at Agnea's open, earnest face. The dancer wore her mother's rose on her headband, and her dress—also her mother's, re-tailored—caught the lantern light. She looked every inch the star she'd become, yet here she was in her home village, asking a former assassin to watch people argue over pastries.

Throné took her hand.

They wove through the crowd together, Agnea leading the way with easy familiarity, greeting neighbors by name and introducing Throné as "my friend who helped me reach the Grand Gala." The villagers welcomed her with the same uncomplicated warmth they showed everyone, asking no questions about her past or her scars.

The contest tent was packed. A long table displayed twelve pies, each labeled with its baker's name. Three judges—including Garud, Agnea's father—sat at the front, sampling each entry with theatrical seriousness. The crowd pressed close, and Throné felt her shoulders tense, but Agnea's hand remained steady in hers.

"That's Mrs. Keeland's entry," Agnea whispered, pointing to a raspberry pie with an elaborate lattice crust. "She's won three years running. But see that one? Mr. Harrow's. He's been experimenting with peach-raspberry combinations. Very controversial."

Throné found herself watching not the pies but the people: the way the bakers fidgeted nervously, the way neighbors teased each other with good-natured rivalry, the way Garud tasted each pie with careful consideration before making notes. This was what Agnea had grown up with. Not darkness and collars and the stench of blood, but this—community, tradition, the fierce importance of fruit-based pastries.

"The raspberry-peach is good," one judge announced, "but it lacks focus. A pie should know what it is."

"Told you it was controversial," Agnea murmured.

Mrs. Keeland won again. The crowd erupted in applause and playful groans. Throné felt the press of bodies around her but found it less suffocating than she'd expected. Maybe because she could leave whenever she wanted. Maybe because Agnea stood beside her, solid and bright as a flame.

As the crowd dispersed, Garud approached, wiping crumbs from his beard. "Agnea. Miss Throné." He nodded respectfully. "I hope you're both enjoying the festival."

"Papa, isn't Throné's dress beautiful?" Agnea said suddenly. "Look at the stitching on the bodice. And the way the purple catches the light."

Garud leaned closer, his tailor's eye assessing the garment with professional interest. "The craftsmanship is excellent. The drape is intentional—it allows freedom of movement while maintaining structure. And that leather work on the bodice..." He nodded approvingly. "Whoever made this understood fabric as well as function."

"I don't know who made it," Throné admitted. "It came from the Blacksnakes' resources. Probably stolen."

"Hmm." Garud stroked his chin. "Stolen or not, the maker had skill. The soul of the artisan lives in good work. You wear it well."

Throné blinked. It was the exact sentiment she'd tried to explain to Pirro once—that clothes carried the souls of their makers. She'd never expected a village tailor in Cropdale to voice the same thought. "Thank you."

"Papa makes the best costumes in the Leaflands," Agnea said proudly. "He made Mama's performance dresses, and this one too." She smoothed her skirt. "Everything I wear on stage, he's touched."

Garud's expression softened. "I do what I can to help my daughter shine. As any father should." He glanced at Throné. "Agnea wrote that you traveled with her through dark times. That you faced the Shadow together. I don't know what that means entirely, but I know my daughter chose her companions well. Thank you for standing beside her."

"She stood beside me first," Throné said quietly. It was more than she usually offered, but the words came easier here.

Garud nodded once, understanding passing between them without further speech. "I should return to judging duties. The raspberry jam contest is next, and people take it even more seriously." He patted Agnea's shoulder and returned to the judges' table.

"See?" Agnea said. "I told you you'd like it here."

"I didn't say I liked it."

"You didn't say you didn't." Agnea grinned. "That's basically the same thing for you."

Throné couldn't quite suppress the small smile that tugged at her mouth. "You're too clever for your own good."

"Well now, I learned from the best." Agnea's country dialect slipped through, the one she worked so hard to hide in cities. Here, surrounded by home, she let it surface naturally. "Come on. I want to show you the orchards before it gets too dark. They're prettiest at sunset."

They walked north past the Festival Grounds, following a well-worn path between rows of cultivated trees. The raspberry bushes grew in neat lines, their fruit already harvested for the festival but their leaves still green and full. Beyond them, peach trees spread their branches wide, a few late fruits still clinging to the highest boughs.

Agnea stopped in the middle of the orchard, turning slowly to take it all in. "I used to practice dancing here when I was younger. The ground is soft, and nobody could hear me if I messed up." She demonstrated a few steps, her heeled shoes sinking slightly into the earth, then spun and nearly lost her balance. Throné caught her elbow.

"Careful."

"See? Still clumsy." But Agnea was smiling. "Mama used to say that stumbling just means you're moving forward. You can't fall if you're standing still."

Throné released her arm slowly. "Your mother sounds like she was wise."

"She was." Agnea touched the rose on her headband, her good luck charm. "She died from working too hard, trying to bring smiles to everyone. Papa was so afraid I'd do the same that he almost didn't let me go." Her voice dropped. "I think he still worries."

"He came to Merry Hills for you."

"He did. And he cheered louder than anyone." Agnea's eyes were bright, but no tears fell. She'd learned, Throné thought, to carry grief without being crushed by it. "I'm lucky. I got to know her, and I have Papa and Pala, and now I have all of you. All eight of us." She turned to Throné. "Do you ever think about your mother? Your real one, I mean."

Throné was quiet for a long moment. The question would have been intrusive from anyone else, but Agnea asked with such gentle sincerity that it didn't feel like prying. "Sometimes. Marietta. I never knew her—Mother killed her before I had memories. But I know she loved Father. Sebastian, I mean. And she tried to run away from the life Claude had planned for her." She looked at the darkening sky. "I think she would have wanted something like this. Peace. Ordinary happiness."

"Then maybe the place you're looking for should have raspberry orchards," Agnea said softly. "So you can think of her when you taste them."

The suggestion was so simple, so Agnea, that Throné almost laughed. Instead, she nodded. "Maybe it should."

They stood together in the orchard as the sun dipped lower, painting the sky in shades of orange and pink. Festival music drifted from the grounds—someone had started playing a fiddle, and voices rose in a folk song Agnea probably knew by heart.

"I should get back," Agnea said eventually. "They'll want me to perform soon. Nothing big—just a song or two. You don't have to watch if you don't want to. I know you've seen me dance plenty of times."

"I'll watch." The words surprised Throné even as she spoke them.

Agnea's face lit up. "Really?"

"Really. But from the back."

"That's fine! That's perfect!" Agnea grabbed both of Throné's hands, squeezing them. "Thank you for coming, Throné. I know it's not easy for you. But I'm really glad you're here."

Throné squeezed back, brief and careful. "So am I."

They returned to the Festival Grounds as the last light faded and the lanterns took over, painting everything in warm gold. True to her word, Throné positioned herself at the back of the crowd, her spine against a tree, exits clear. But she stayed, watching as Agnea took the stage to enthusiastic applause. 

The dancer performed three songs—two of her mother's and then the Song of Hope that had won her recognition at the Grand Gala. Her voice carried clearly across the grounds, bright and warm, and her steps never faltered. When she stumbled slightly on the final turn, she turned it into a playful spin that drew laughter and cheers.

Throné watched a village celebrate its daughter. She watched people smile and clap not because they feared consequences or calculated advantage, but because they were genuinely happy. And she thought about raspberry orchards and quiet places and the strange, fragile thing that Agnea called friendship.

When Agnea found her after the performance, breathless and glowing, Throné was still there.

"So?" Agnea asked, hope and nervousness mixing in her voice. "What did you think?"

"You didn't trip."

"I almost did!"

"But you didn't." Throné allowed herself a full smile, small but genuine. "You were good. The Song of Hope suits you."

Agnea beamed. "I'm glad you heard it here. In Cropdale. Where it all started." She linked her arm through Throné's in an easy, familiar gesture. "Come on. Pala saved us both slices of Mrs. Keeland's pie. We can eat them by the bridge—the view is pretty, and the curse lady doesn't come out until midnight."

Throné let herself be led toward the bridge, toward raspberry pie and Pala's teasing and the gentle rhythm of a village festival. Toward something that felt, for one evening, almost like the peace she'd been searching for.

Maybe when she found her hidden place—her sanctuary absent from every map—she would plant raspberry bushes. And maybe, sometimes, Agnea would visit, bringing her bright smile and her clumsy steps and her unshakeable belief that every sunrise brought another chance to shine.

For now, though, this was enough. This was good.

"Throné?" Agnea's voice was soft. "You're smiling."

"Hmm." Throné didn't deny it. "The raspberries here really are the best in Solistia."

Agnea laughed, the sound carrying across the Festival Grounds like a song, and Throné found she didn't mind at all.
